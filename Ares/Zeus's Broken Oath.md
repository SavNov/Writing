After the age of Titans, the age of Giants, and after so much of the age of Gods, Zeus allowed himself to remember the last time he had tempted fate.
World War 2 had broken out. Children of the eldest gods fighting. In hindsight, it seemed incredibly foolish. It was nearly the spitting image of the war that had broken out a century before. Gods fighting, children of those gods fighting, divided by their natures and fragmented along sides.
Children of the sea god lying in boats on June 6 as German machine guns on the Normandy shore drowned them in bullets. Any of them would've been dead, but they were demigods. The water of the ocean spray healed them even as it soaked with the blood of their friends and brothers. German soldiers, some mortal, some monsters, some demigods fighting for what they viewed as the legacy of Germany and its great gods.
Children of Hades trying to survive the death camps, some German for what they called the long-nosers, some American for the Japs, sensing each execution long before they heard about it as monsters and demigods fought side by side to rid their country of the plague within its borders. If only they knew what Jews meant when they called their God long-of-nose.
Patient. Slow to anger.
One mortal man without a drop of divine blood managing to do more damage to the continent than the whole century of demigod quests before him.
German Luftwaffe pilots, mothers he'd taken a chance on in the midst of a country being actively buried by its own leadership, now summoning lightning from the Heavens above to strike down RAF pilots playing the same game they were.
Because three immortal, perpetually bickering brother kings had not been able to learn the lesson the American Civil War had broken out to teach them:
A fight among children of the Big Three cannot be won. Just as a fight between the Big Three couldn't be won.
The only ending to a real war among the gods.

So they'd made the pact. Sworn the oaths to each other. No more children of the eldest gods. Three kings had realized there was no point in being a king without a kingdom.
And then, fifty years later, a young Sally Jackson had won the heart of the king of the sea. He had risked his whole kingdom to have a son. On the same day that Percy Jackson had been born, the Oracle of Delphi had cried her song:
