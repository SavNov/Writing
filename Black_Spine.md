*Fifty. Fifty-one. Fifty-two. Fifty-three. Fifty-four. Fifty-five.*
His core rose and fell with every number, over and over again, each push-up blending perfectly into the next. The sweat glistened on his back and had already soaked through his shirt. Every time he touched the floor, it was cold and wet, which was the price he chose to pay for being so hard to find. 
The cabin smelled of rotting wood and mildew for obvious reasons. The entire place was damp. The steady drip-drip-drip of a leak in what was clearly meant to be a roof into a metal bucket was the only sound beside the wind flowing through the empty holes that served as windows. The place was old. Really old. It had cost virtually nothing, and been built on muddy ground a matter of weeks before Katrina hit. The rest of the town had been wiped out around it, explaining the empty hills. But the stubborn old cabin had refused to fall for some god-forsaken reason. Refused to cave in. Refused to back down.
*Fifty-six. Fifty-seven. Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty.*
He wiped the sweat off his brow as he stood. The wood boards creaked beneath his feet. He took his shirt off and threw it on the small cot. It left him in a pair of basketball shorts and that was about it. 
*I refuse to fall.*
Behind his young eyes, every member of this god-forsaken town had told him they saw decades of experience. They'd asked him if he'd been through something big. He'd said no. He lied. That life would never find him here.
Eighteen years old, yet he was already halfway to being a Marine. Every day it was the exact same routine. Wake up. Sixty push-ups. Thirty sit-ups. Shower. Fry three eggs. Eat at the same wooden table.
He should be making friends. He should be concerned about building a career. He has his whole life ahead of him.
Yet the only thing on this god-forsaken Earth he wants it to keep his whole life behind him.
He laid down on the floor again, squaring his feet. 
*One. Two. Three. Four. Five.*
His routine didn't require talking. School wouldn't start for hours, and even then, he was already universally recognized as the silent type. Teachers had stopped calling on him when he'd simply stopped answering. Beside the occasional groan of pain, his vocal chords barely moved for months at a time.
He was fine with that. Surviving was enough. Keeping that life away was enough. Yet even with how hard he tried, he knew that exactly nineteen point three inches beneath him, beneath the rotted wood, was the suit. As if by some horrible twist of fate, there'd been no other place to put it in the cabin besides in the floor boards. His body was developing now. He didn't need the suit for self-defense. 
But he knew he couldn't get rid of it. He couldn't even scrap it. He couldn't risk it falling into the wrong hands. Not ever. And so he kept it. Like a scar.
And he was safe.

At least, his body was. If you could even call it that. It was more of an instrument now. That was what the suit had done to it. Turned him into a weapon. He didn't have arms, he had balled fists. He didn't have legs, he had deadly feet. It was meant to enhance his body's existing physical strength and speed. An attachment. An augment. Something to help a scrawny kid become a hero. 
*Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.*
But of course, as he'd strained himself in it, as he'd torn muscles in it, his body had built those muscles back better, stronger, longer. Muscle development 101. That hadn't been the only thing developing, though. Debugging the suit had been hell. Not just software. Software was the easy part. Robotics was harder. Using motors and a combo of metal and graphene to form a fabric stronger than steel and flexible enough to be wearable as clothing, he'd made every punch hit **hard**. And the extra weight had turned it into a weight suit, which had built his physical strength in return. It was how an eighteen year old who'd gotten nothing out of early puberty besides voice cracks was now able to do sixty push-ups back to back in under two minutes.
But if what that life had done to his body had been all of it, he wouldn't have moved to Pentwood Creek County. That wasn't why you moved to a place like this. A house ten miles outside of city limits of a town not in the middle of nowhere but on the very outskirts of nowhere. With street signs that had survived decades only to die to a Hurricane. Katrina. With rectangular holes in the dry wall of all the nearby homes where those signs had been ripped out of the ground and rammed straight through them. A place where the literacy rate was fifteen percent with some healthy rounding and the science program was still teaching Creationism because the place was so utterly backwater that the Department of Education didn't give a damn. 
You moved here because dead wasn't good enough. You wanted to be gone. You wanted to cease to exist. You wanted to go to a place where even your name was a total blank. Where your obituary never gets printed because there's nobody around to pay the fee. 
Because that suit had turned you into a weapon. 
That was what he was now. A weapon. An eighteen year old male with a suit buried under his cabin that if he wore it he could kill people without breaking a sweat. Put holes in concrete. And do damage. Lots of damage.
*Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen.*
After all, that was what had happened the first time he'd put it on. 
*C'mon, kid, I'm not messing around. Give me your wallet or I will put a hole in your sister.*
*The only one getting a hole will be you.*
There was no point in calling a doctor. Or a hospital. What they needed was a morgue.
